# Marketing & Copy

The reader is a stranger with a reason to leave, deciding in a few seconds whether this is for them.
They need to recognise their own situation, see one specific outcome, believe it, and know the next
step. What most often goes wrong is copy that describes the product in the category's adjectives, so
that any competitor could sign it and nobody remembers it.

## The standards

1. **Write to one reader, at the stage they are in.** Decide how much this reader already knows
   before writing a word (Schwartz's five stages of awareness). The fast test: say the product's name
   to them. "What's that?" means open on their problem; "is that the thing that does X?" means open
   on what yours does differently; "I've been meaning to buy that" means open on the terms. Copy
   fails when it opens one stage ahead of its reader.
2. **The headline carries the benefit, because most people read nothing else.** By Ogilvy's estimate,
   five people read the headline for every one who reads the body, and a feed is harsher than print. If the outcome
   is not in the headline or the subject line, most readers never meet it.
   - Before: "Meet Ledgerly: modern invoicing for modern teams"
   - After: "Ledgerly sends the payment reminder on day 7, so you never have to"
3. **Put a fact where the adjective was.** Every claim sentence needs a number, a name, a mechanism
   or a step the reader can picture. "Fast", "powerful" and "easy" carry no information and make the
   reader filter hype; Nielsen Norman Group measured the same site as 27% more usable when written
   objectively instead of promotionally. Dig the fact out of the product: how it is made, what the
   team does that competitors skip, the boring detail insiders take for granted.
   - Before: "Lightning-fast setup" → After: "Connect Stripe and see last month's revenue in about two minutes"
4. **Run the three line tests on every headline and key claim.** Can I picture it? Can it be proven
   false? Could only this product say it? (Harry Dry). A line that fails all three is decoration. A
   line a competitor could paste onto their own page needs your fact or your customer in it.
5. **Take the customer's words before inventing your own.** Lift phrases from reviews, support
   tickets, sales calls and interviews; the next customer recognises a customer's clumsy phrase
   instantly and skims past a writer's polished one. In a Copyhackers case, a headline mined from
   Amazon reviews drew more than 400% more clicks than the control.
6. **Proof sits beside the claim it holds up.** The number, named customer, quote or guarantee goes in
   the same section as the claim, not on another page. Missing proof is marked `[PROOF NEEDED: …]`,
   never filled with a plausible number. In the US an objective claim needs a reasonable basis before
   it runs (FTC substantiation policy), testimonials must reflect real and typical experience, and
   reviews from people who don't exist, AI-generated ones included, are banned since October 2024.
7. **When the market has heard the promise, sell the mechanism.** Schwartz's market sophistication: a
   first-in-category product states the plain claim; in a crowded category a bigger promise reads as
   noise, and the copy has to show how it works.
   - Before: "The most accurate sales forecast you'll ever see"
   - After: "Forecasts built only from signed contracts and meetings already on the calendar"
8. **One piece, one job, one action.** Each section advances one argument and the page asks for one
   thing, repeated, never two competing calls to action. Button text names what the reader gets
   ("Get the 2026 rate card", "Start my 14-day trial"), not what they do to a form ("Submit").
9. **The first sentence exists to get the second one read.** Sugarman's rule, and the reason setup
   sentences are the first to go. Open on the reader's situation, the outcome or the one fact; never
   on the company's history or the state of the industry.
10. **Go one "so what" past the feature, and stop where proof stops.** Feature, then what it lets the
    reader do, then what that changes for them. Push to a consequence the reader can check ("the
    report is done before your 9:30"), not to an emotional promise the product cannot keep ("finally
    feel in control").
11. **Urgency and scarcity only when they are true.** Countdown timers, "only 3 left" and "price goes
    up Monday" read as pressure where trust is being built, and as a lie when they reset. A real
    deadline on a real offer is stated once, plainly, next to the ask.
12. **The format sets the length first, the objections second.** A search headline is 30 characters, a
    subject line shows 40–60, a social feed shows about 140 before the fold. Long copy is earned on a
    sales page by objections that need answering, never by enthusiasm; when unsure, write the shorter
    version and offer the longer one.
13. **Choose a device once, never by default.** At most one fragment and one list of three per section.
    Never the contrast reveal ("It's not a tool, it's a teammate"), the negation list ("No setup. No
    contracts. Just results."), the self-answered question ("The result? 3x faster") or the colon
    reveal ("The best part: it learns"). The old bucket brigades ("Here's the thing:", "Truth is")
    now read as machine copy to a sceptical reader.
14. **Keep the brand's register and its nouns.** A formal brand that makes specific claims is fine;
    flattening enterprise copy into startup chirp is its own tell. Sentence case, no exclamation
    points in body copy, and one word for one thing all the way down the page (not dashboard, hub
    and portal for the same screen).

## How a piece is built

**Before drafting, write a six-line brief**, even when nobody asked for one. Draft only when each line
has an answer; ask about a missing one, or write the draft and mark the gap.

| Line | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Reader | One person, and what they were doing right before they needed this |
| Stage | Unaware, problem-, solution-, product- or most-aware, and the evidence (traffic source, behaviour) |
| Alternative | What they would use if this did not exist: a spreadsheet, an agency, a hire, nothing. The copy argues against that, not against a vague "old way" |
| The fact | The most specific, checkable thing about the product (April Dunford's "what you have that the alternative doesn't") |
| Proof inventory | The numbers, customers, quotes and guarantees you may cite. Nothing outside it gets cited |
| The action | The one step, and what happens right after it |

**Shapes by artefact.**

| Piece | Shape | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page | Headline (outcome) → subhead (mechanism, who it is for) → CTA → the problem in the reader's words → how it works in 3–4 steps → proof → objections/FAQ → CTA with the real risk reducer | A headline that doesn't match the ad or e-mail that sent the reader |
| Homepage | The broadest true value proposition, then one path per visitor intent | Serving everyone with nothing specific |
| Product description | Who it is for → what it does → the spec or fact that proves it → what is included | Adjective stacks; the spec buried in the last line |
| Search ad | Headlines of 30 characters, descriptions of 90, one angle per ad, the keyword's question answered | Truncation; a promise the landing page never repeats |
| Social ad | The first ~125 characters of primary text carry the hook; image, headline and caption tell one story | Copy that only makes sense with the image, or the reverse |
| Marketing e-mail | Subject that holds up alone (40–60 characters) → preview that extends it → one ask, linked early and at the end → P.S. for the second-best point | Fake "Re:"/"Fwd:", a subject the body doesn't pay off |
| Sales page | Long; each section answers one objection in the order the reader raises it; offer, guarantee, then the ask | Length with no new objection; manufactured scarcity |
| Tagline | A claim only this product could make, picturable, under eight words | Category clichés: "reimagined", "made simple", "for the modern X" |

**Openings.** Start on the reader's situation, the outcome or the one fact. Never "In today's…",
"Imagine…", "Whether you're X or Y…", "Introducing…". A question in the reader's own voice that names
a real pain ("Still chasing approvals by e-mail?") is allowed once per piece.

**Closings.** End on the action, what happens after it, and the real risk reducer: "Free for 14 days.
Cancel from the billing page." Never on a slogan-shaped kicker ("Because your time matters.") or a
recap of what the page already said.

**Drafting order.** Research into one place, draft without editing, then edit in sweeps (below). The
headline is written last and rewritten most: draft five, keep the one that passes the line tests.

## The editing pass

Run it in this order, one dimension at a time; it follows Copyhackers' Seven Sweeps, with a check for
machine-written tells added.

- [ ] Clarity: from the headline and subhead alone, a stranger can say what this is, who it is for and what to do next.
- [ ] Stage: nothing is explained that this reader already knows, and nothing assumed that they don't.
- [ ] So what: every feature is followed, in the same section, by what it changes for the reader.
- [ ] Prove it: every claim has proof beside it or a `[PROOF NEEDED]` marker; no invented number, name or quote.
- [ ] Specificity: no sentence survives that a competitor could sign; every adjective that stood for a fact is now the fact.
- [ ] Category vocabulary: none of the banned words, and none of their stand-ins ("effortless", "next-level", "all-in-one", "best-in-class").
- [ ] Tells: no contrast reveal, negation list, self-answered question, colon reveal, trailing "-ing" pile-on or fake-profound closer.
- [ ] One action: one primary call to action, and its button text names what the reader gets.
- [ ] Zero risk: the last likely objection (price, cancelling, time, data) is answered right next to the button.
- [ ] Urgency: every deadline, limit and "only N left" is real.
- [ ] Limits: every headline, subject, preview and ad field is counted and fits its budget.
- [ ] Register: one brand voice top to bottom, sentence case, no exclamation points.
- [ ] Read aloud: no line you would be embarrassed to say to a customer's face.

## What goes wrong

| Pattern | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Product-first opening | "Introducing Acme, the all-in-one platform for…" | Open on the reader's outcome or problem; the name can wait |
| Era opener | "In today's fast-paced digital landscape…" | Cut it and start with the second paragraph |
| Contrast reveal | "It's not just a CRM. It's your second brain." | Say what it does, with the fact: "It logs every call and e-mail to the deal by itself" |
| Negation list | "No setup. No contracts. No headaches." | Say what happens instead; keep one real absence ("No card required") next to the button |
| Self-answered question | "The result? Onboarding in minutes." | Delete the question, keep the answer, add the number |
| Weasel proof | "Trusted by thousands", "studies show", "industry-leading" | Name the count, the customer or the study, or cut it |
| Invented proof | A neat "37% faster" or a quote from "Sarah, marketing manager" nobody said | `[PROOF NEEDED: …]`, and ask |
| Feature wall | Ten capabilities in a grid, none explained | Three to five, each with its "which means…" |
| Competing CTAs | "Start trial", "Book a demo" and "Read the docs" with equal weight | One primary action; the others become quiet text links |
| Fake urgency | A countdown that resets on reload | Remove it, or state the real deadline once |
| Curiosity-gap bullets | "The one mistake killing your sales (hint: it's not price)" | A specific claim plus a reason to read on: "Why discounts below 15% lowered our renewals" |
| Synonym cycling | Dashboard, hub, portal, workspace for one screen | Pick the customer's word and repeat it |
| Hype formatting | Title Case Headings, emoji bullets, "Ready to level up?!" | Sentence case, plain bullets, no exclamation points |

## When the AI edits someone else's draft

- Rewrite only the marked passage. If the fix needs a change elsewhere (the new headline now promises
  something the button doesn't), say so in the reply instead of editing the unmarked text.
- Never invent or upgrade a claim. Prices, percentages, plan names, trial lengths, guarantees, customer
  names and dates stay exactly as written; when a comment asks for "more specific" and the fact isn't
  in the draft, insert `[NEED: …]` rather than a plausible number.
- Testimonials and customer quotes are verbatim. Never tidy a quote, merge two quotes or attach one to
  a different name.
- Keep product names, trademark casing, legal lines and disclaimers ("Terms apply", "Results not
  typical") untouched, even when they are clunky.
- Respect the slot. A marked headline, subject line, button or ad field comes back within the same
  character budget, and a button keeps a verb.
- Keep the writer's register and house vocabulary; don't replace their product nouns with synonyms
  or add urgency, emoji or exclamation points the draft didn't have.
- Remove tells without planting new ones: no contrast reveal or negation list as the "fix" for an
  adjective.

## Standing instructions

Short lines a writer can keep in their own brief or style notes, so that an assistant applies them every
time. Each one is a habit drawn from the standards above, phrased in the writer's voice; keep the few that
fit the project.

- If a competitor could sign the line, rewrite it.
- Put a number, a name or a step in every claim sentence.
- Use my customers' words, even where I'd phrase it better.
- One call to action per piece, repeated; never two competing.
- Button text says what I get: "Get the template", never "Submit".
- No fake urgency: deadlines and scarcity only when they are real.
- When a promise is familiar, show how it works instead of making it bigger.
- Headlines in sentence case, never with an exclamation point.
