# Scripts & Speeches

A script is read twice: first by someone deciding whether to make it (a producer, a director, an actor, the
speaker at rehearsal), then by an audience that hears it once, in real time, with no way to go back a line.
Both need pages that play: an image someone can shoot, a line someone can say, a structure someone can follow
by ear. What goes wrong most often is prose written for the eye: a sentence that reads well and cannot be said,
an action line describing a feeling no camera can photograph, a speech that sounds like an essay read aloud.

## The standards

1. **The page is a clock.** One screenplay page is about one minute; a speech runs 130–150 spoken words a
   minute. Every length decision is a time decision, because the producer budgets by page and the organizer
   by minute. An edit that adds half a page adds thirty seconds somebody has to pay for.
2. **A scene earns its place by turning something.** Name the value at stake at the top (trust, safety, the
   deal) and where it stands at the end. If nothing changed, it is exposition: cut it or fold it into a scene
   that turns. A scene that does only one job (plot, character, world) is a candidate for cutting.
3. **Enter late, leave before the conflict resolves.** Start after the hellos and the parking; cut while the
   question is still open, so the next scene inherits its pressure. The same rule shrinks a single speech.
4. **Every line of dialogue is an action.** A character says something to get something. If you cannot name
   the verb under the line (stalling, pleading, testing, punishing), the line is talk, not drama.
5. **Subtext stays under the line.** People rarely say what they feel; they say what they want, or what
   hides it. Before: *"I'm hurt that you forgot my birthday."* After: *"Nice of you to come. Long drive?"*
   The exceptions are the blunt questions people really ask under pressure: "What do you want?"
6. **Exposition is ammunition.** Nobody tells someone a fact both already know ("As you know, we've been
   partners for twenty years"). Give the fact to a character who needs it to win the exchange, and hold the
   biggest piece back until it costs someone something to say.
7. **Cover the names.** Hide the character cues; you should still know who is talking from vocabulary, rhythm
   and what they notice. If everyone sounds like the writer, nobody is anyone yet.
8. **Action lines are images, not descriptions.** Concrete nouns, specific verbs (*shuffles*, not *walks
   slowly*), no adjectives the camera cannot see. Four lines at most per paragraph; a blank line reads as a new
   shot. No "we see", no camera angles and no music cues in a spec script: the image is the instruction.
9. **Directions are rationed.** A parenthetical exists when a line would otherwise be misread or to say who
   is being addressed; three words, a verb. In a play, stage directions say what the audience can see or hear,
   because actors and directors own the rest.
10. **A talk carries one idea the room can repeat.** If the speaker cannot say it in one sentence, the
    audience cannot remember it. Everything else (stories, data, the joke) is there to make that sentence
    land. Three body points is the ceiling, not the target.
11. **Open on the stake; close on the line.** The first thirty seconds go to a story, a question or a promise
    of what the listener will be able to do afterward, never to logistics or thanks. The last words are the
    line you want quoted. "Thank you" after it is the audience's cue to clap, not the ending.
12. **Signpost out loud.** The ear cannot see headings. Say "Three reasons," "That was the first," "Here is
    where it changed." Say the central idea at least three times, each time in different words, because some
    of the room was elsewhere the first time.
13. **Put the weight at the end of the sentence.** The last word is the one the listener keeps and the one
    the other actor reacts to. Before: *"Courage is what, more than anything, this moment demands."* After:
    *"What this moment demands is courage."*
14. **Numbers, names and sources are said, not printed.** Round them ("nearly one and a half million," not
    "$1,347,892"), one figure per sentence, attribution before the claim ("Police say the driver fled"), a
    title before a name the room does not know.
15. **Small words carry big things.** "The most moving thing in a speech is its logic" (Noonan). Utilize,
    leverage and paradigm do not survive being spoken; a concrete image and a short sentence do.
16. **On video and audio, every beat names what we see or hear.** A video script marks what is on screen
    beside what is said; the opening fifteen seconds confirm what the title promised. In audio, alternate a
    run of events with a moment of reflection (Ira Glass's two building blocks), and never let the line that
    introduces a clip repeat what the clip says.

## How a piece is built

Before drafting, answer four questions in a line each: who hears or reads this first; how long it runs; what
is different for them at the end; what the one sentence is (logline for a story, throughline for a talk).

| Kind | Unit | Length | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Feature screenplay | scene, sequence | 90–120 pages | Inciting incident by p. 12–15, commitment by ~25, midpoint ~55, low point ~75, finale; the last image answers the first (Snyder's beat sheet as a check, not a mold) |
| TV episode | act | half-hour 25–35 pp, hour 50–65 pp | Cold open or teaser, acts that each end on a question (the act out), A/B/C stories that touch thematically |
| Stage play | scene, act | 80–120 minutes | Continuous time inside a scene, few places, offstage events reported; directions indented and set apart |
| Short film | one scene or a few | 5–15 pages | One conflict, one turn, one payoff; no setup the length cannot pay |
| Video script | beat | ~150 words a minute | Hook that confirms the title, the turn (what this video will do) by 0:45, beats with an on-screen cue, the promised payoff said explicitly, one ask |
| Documentary / explainer | segment | per brief | Two-column A/V layout: picture left, sound and narration right; narration writes to the picture, not over it |
| Podcast | segment | per show | Cold open on the best tape, host intro, segments of anecdote and reflection, out-roll; in two-host formats one teaches and one asks, a turn is at most three sentences |
| Talk / keynote | movement | 18–40 minutes | Hook, why this and why me in under a minute, the one idea, 2–4 movements each built on a story, callback to the hook, close |
| Toast / eulogy / award | moment | 1–7 minutes | One specific story, not a list of traits; toast ends on the raised glass, eulogy on what the person leaves, acceptance thanks fast then says one thing bigger than thanks |

**Screen and stage workflow.** Logline in one sentence → step outline, one line per scene (whose scene, what
turns) → draft fast → table read aloud → cut. Fix structure in the outline; a dialogue problem is often a
story problem one level up.

**Speech workflow.** Intake: speaker, audience, occasion, goal, time limit, must-mention and must-not-mention
→ the one sentence → pick an engine (narrative, problem–solution, Monroe's attention–need–satisfaction–
visualization–action, or thematic) → draft for the mouth, one thought per line → read it aloud with a timer →
cut to 90% of the slot, because live delivery runs long. Persuasive talks alternate *what is* with *what could
be* (Duarte) and plant one moment the room will retell.

## The editing pass

- [ ] Each scene states, to you, what turns in it; scenes where nothing turns are cut or merged.
- [ ] Every scene starts after its beginning and leaves before its conflict closes.
- [ ] No line tells someone what they already know; no character announces their own feeling.
- [ ] With the names covered, each speaker is identifiable.
- [ ] Action paragraphs are four lines or fewer, present tense, with no camera language and no "we see".
- [ ] Parentheticals and stage directions are only where a line would be misread without them.
- [ ] Page count or word count matches the slot; the draft was read aloud and timed.
- [ ] The talk's one idea is a single sentence and appears near the start, in the middle and at the close.
- [ ] The opening is a story, question or promise; the last words are the line meant to be remembered.
- [ ] Numbers are rounded and said; attribution comes before the claim; no acronym is left unspoken.
- [ ] Every video or A/V beat names what is on screen; every audio clip is introduced without being repeated.
- [ ] Setups have payoffs and callbacks still match their originals word for word.
- [ ] Format is intact: headings, cues, extensions, transitions and blank lines exactly where the writer put them.

## What goes wrong

- **Novel in disguise.** "She remembers the summer before everything changed." → what the actor can play:
  "She stops at the door. Looks at the place where the couch used to be. Doesn't go in."
- **Directing on the page.** "CLOSE ON her hand. We PUSH IN as..." → write the hand: "Her hand. The ring is
  gone." The reader sees the shot without being told to.
- **Therapy dialogue.** "I feel like you never really hear me." → the behaviour: he keeps typing; she closes
  the laptop on his hands.
- **As-you-know exposition.** Two characters reciting shared history → put the fact in a fight, a joke or a
  lie, where knowing it helps one of them.
- **Everyone speaks in finished sentences.** Real speech interrupts, backtracks and leaves things out; tidy
  grammar in every mouth is the clearest machine tell in a script.
- **AI tells in scripts:** "something shifts in her eyes", "a mix of fear and determination", "the silence is
  deafening", "(A BEAT.)" after every line, scenes that end on an aphorism, villains who explain the theme.
  Fix: one observable action, or nothing.
- **AI tells in talks:** "Today I want to talk to you about", "We stand at a crossroads", "In a world where",
  "Let that sink in", three stacked tricolons, "journey", and a summary that repeats every point before
  "thank you". Fix: open on the story, cut the recap, end on the line.
- **The logistics opening.** Thanks, housekeeping and an agenda in the first minute → move the thanks after
  the hook, or into a single sentence.
- **The résumé toast.** Seven adjectives about the couple → one story that shows one of them.
- **Five points.** The room keeps three at most; merge or cut, and say how many there are.
- **Spreadsheet numbers.** "A 37.2% increase across 14 of 19 regions" → "Up more than a third, almost
  everywhere."
- **The creator preamble.** "Hey everyone, welcome back, before we start, smash that like button" ahead of
  the hook → hook first; the ask goes at the end, once.
- **Parroting the tape.** "The mayor said the plan was a disaster." / MAYOR: "This plan is a disaster." →
  set up who and why: "The mayor didn't wait for the vote."

## When the AI edits someone else's draft

- **The format is the writer's, character for character.** Keep scene headings, character cues, extensions
  such as (V.O.), (O.S.) and (CONT'D), transitions, page breaks, Fountain markers (`.` `@` `>` `!` `~` `=`
  `#` `[[ ]]` `===` and centred `> <`), the two A/V columns, bracketed delivery cues such as [pause], and blank
  lines: in a script a blank line is structure, not spacing.
- **Rewrite only the element that was marked.** A note on a line of dialogue changes that line, not the action
  around it; never merge two speeches, split one, or move a line to another character.
- **Keep the length.** A rewrite of a marked passage stays within a line or two of the original, and a speech
  edit stays near its word count. If the note needs more time than the slot allows, say so instead.
- **Keep the voice of the mouth that says it.** Dialect spellings, slang, fragments and a speaker's pet
  phrases stay; a character's grammar is characterization, not a mistake.
- **Never invent what will be said in public.** No new statistic, quote, name, anecdote or applause line in a
  speech: a real person will stand up and own it.
- **Do not add direction.** No new camera moves, parentheticals, music cues or stage business the writer did
  not write.
- **Protect setups and callbacks.** If the marked line plants something paid off later (a prop, a phrase, a
  running joke), keep its key words; if they must change, say which later line now needs to match.
- **Respect the occasion.** No jokes into a eulogy, no solemnity into a roast, no sales pitch into a toast.

## Standing instructions

Short lines a writer can keep in their own brief or style notes, so that an assistant applies them every
time. Each one is a habit drawn from the standards above, phrased in the writer's voice; keep the few that
fit the project.

- Enter every scene late and leave it before the conflict resolves.
- Keep each action paragraph to four lines or fewer.
- No "we see", no camera angles: put the image itself on the page.
- Characters never name their own feelings; let what they do show it.
- Cover the names: every character should sound like nobody else.
- Open a talk with a story or a stake, never with thanks or logistics.
- Give every talk one idea the audience could repeat over dinner.
- End on the line you want remembered, not on a recap.
