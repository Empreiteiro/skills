# Newsletters

A newsletter is read in an inbox, between a bank alert and a calendar invite, by someone who decides from three
lines — sender, subject, preview — whether to open it, and then gives it about a minute: Nielsen Norman Group
measured an average of 51 seconds per newsletter, with 19% read in full. The reader needs to feel it was written to
them, by someone, and that the open paid off. The common failure is the issue written as a broadcast: a greeting, a
table of contents and a recap of what the sender did, addressed to everyone and so to no one.

## The standards

1–4 are the core standards, the short list to hand a writer or a model. 5 onward extend them: they are what a
reviewer checks.

1. **Write to one person.** Second person, contractions, no announcements to an audience. Ann Handley's version:
   one person, one inbox, a letter rather than a broadcast. "Hi all" and "Dear readers" are plural before the
   first word.
2. **The first line is the whole pitch — it competes with an inbox, not with a page.** Many clients show it as the
   preview when none is set, so it can't be a greeting, a date or "View in browser".
3. **Keep it scannable:** short paragraphs, and a break wherever the subject turns.
4. **End on one thing to do, think, or read**, never on a summary of what was just said.
5. **One idea per issue.** State it in a sentence before writing. Anything else is secondary and looks secondary:
   shorter, lower, under its own label. Three ideas at equal weight are three issues.
6. **Write the subject and the preview text as a pair.** The subject names one concrete thing inside; the preview
   adds new information and never repeats the subject. Keep the preview under about 90 characters, with the point
   in the first 40 (Litmus).
   - Before: "Issue #47: some thoughts on pricing" / "In this issue: pricing, links and more"
   - After: "Why I raised my rate to $40 an hour" / "Two clients left; the other nine paid without asking."
7. **Tell, don't sell, what's inside** (Mailchimp). Curiosity is fine only when the body pays it off in the first
   screen; bait wins one open and spends the reader's habit of opening. No fake "Re:", no "You won't believe", no
   capitals for volume.
8. **Open with the reason you're writing.** Not "Hope this finds you well", not "Happy Friday!", not "Welcome to
   issue #47", not an apology for being late: the reader doesn't need the meta.
9. **Give every link a take** — a sentence or two on why this reader should spend the click. A bare list of links is
   a chore, not a newsletter; the take is the part only this writer can send.
10. **Keep the structure from issue to issue.** Same sections, same order, same names. Readers learn where things
    are, so a change should be a decision, not drift.
11. **Write for the moment it's read.** An issue is opened hours or days after it's written, often in another time
    zone, and "today", "this morning" and "yesterday" go wrong. Use the weekday or the date.
12. **Ask for a reply only with a specific question, and say what you'll do with the answers.** Dan Oshinsky's rule:
    only ask if you give something back. "Hit reply and say YES" teaches readers to ignore every ask after it.
13. **Sound like the name in the From line.** The sender name is the first thing read and the most trusted
    (Oshinsky). A person writes as that person; a team says "we" only when a team really wrote it.
14. **Make it work with images off and on a phone.** Say in words what an image shows, never make "see the chart
    below" carry the point, and cut links that don't serve the issue's one action (Mailchimp: most email is read on
    mobile, and stray links invite stray taps).
15. **Only send when there's something to say** — Mailchimp's oldest rule. A short issue with one good thing beats
    a padded one that keeps the schedule.

## How a piece is built

**Before drafting.** Reread what the reader signed up for (the promise on the signup page) and keep it. Write the
issue's one idea in a sentence and pick its one action: reply, read, try or buy. Choose the format. Draft the
subject line early: once the promise is known, every paragraph can serve it.

**What the reader sees, in order.** Dan Oshinsky counts three things before the open; the rest decide whether the
next issue gets opened too.

| Element | Its job | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| From name | Say who's talking, and earn trust | it's a no-reply address, or changes between issues |
| Subject | Name one concrete thing inside | it's an issue number, a pun with no subject, or hype |
| Preview text | Give a second reason to open | it repeats the subject or leaks "View this email in your browser" |
| First line | Start the payoff | it's a greeting, a date or a table of contents |
| Body | Deliver the one idea | it covers five things at equal weight |
| Close | One thing to do, think or read | it recaps, or stacks four asks |
| Sign-off | The writer's own, short | it's invented, or it's "Stay tuned!" |

**Formats.**

| Format | Works when | Shape | The trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Letter, single essay | the writer's thinking is the product | one idea in 300–800 words, built like a short essay (see `writing-essays`) | a blog post with "Hi friends" on top |
| Curated links | the writer's taste is the product | 3–10 links, each with a take; an optional short lead | bare links; "Great read!" as the take |
| Hybrid | a weekly cadence has to be sustainable | a short lead piece, then 3–5 links with takes | the links bury the lead |
| News briefing | readers want to be caught up fast | top item first, then items in order of consequence, one paragraph each | neutral summaries that never say why it matters |
| Company or team update | a team tells its people or users what changed | what changed for the reader first, sections by area, links to the source documents | a list of everything the team did |

**Openings that work:** the news ("The meetup moves to Thursday."), the moment ("At 2 a.m. the import finished, and
it was wrong."), the claim ("Most onboarding emails are written for the company."). **Closings that work:** one
question the writer wants answered, one link, one thing to try this week. A recurring sign-off is fine when it's the
writer's own.

**Length.** Shorter than it feels. Jakob Nielsen: "The rule for web content is to keep it short. The rule for email
content is to keep it ultra-short." A quick note runs 150–300 words; a letter-essay 300–800. A longer issue works
when the format promised it and the subject line is honest about it.

## The editing pass

- [ ] Read the From name, subject and preview together as one inbox row: would this reader open it? Does the
      preview add to the subject rather than repeat it?
- [ ] The subject's promise is paid within the first screen on a phone.
- [ ] The first line would still work if the platform used it as the preview text.
- [ ] The issue's idea fits in one sentence, and secondary items look secondary.
- [ ] Every "you" means one reader; no "all of you", "subscribers" or "everyone".
- [ ] Every link has a take, and links that don't serve the one action are gone.
- [ ] Paragraphs run one to three sentences on a phone, with a break at every turn.
- [ ] Time words ("today", "this week") are checked against the send date and the readers' time zones.
- [ ] Every image has words around it that make the point without it.
- [ ] Merge tags, links and dates were checked in a test send.
- [ ] The close is one action: no recap, no stack of asks.
- [ ] Sections and their names match the previous issues, or the change is deliberate.
- [ ] Read aloud, it sounds like the sender talking to one person.

## What goes wrong

| Pattern | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Inbox small talk | "Hope this email finds you well!", "Happy Friday, friends!" | The reason you're writing, first. |
| Table of contents | "In this issue: pricing, three links and a recipe" | Start the first item; the reader will scroll. |
| Announcer voice | "We're thrilled to announce…" | Say what changed for the reader. |
| Plural address | "Hi all", "Dear readers", "many of you have asked" | One person: "you asked". |
| Echo preview | Subject "Pricing", preview "This week: pricing" | New information in the preview. |
| Leaked preview | "View this email in your browser" as the visible line | Write the preview, or make the first line the pitch. |
| Bait subject | "Re: your account", "Big news!!", "You won't believe this" | A specific the body pays off. |
| Link dump | Ten links, no opinions | Fewer links, each with a take. |
| Recap close | "That's a wrap! To sum up this week…" | One thing to do, think or read. |
| Ask stack | Reply, share, follow, buy and upgrade | One ask; the rest can wait. |
| Engagement bait | "Hit reply and say YES so I know you got this" | A real question, or no ask at all. |
| Apology opener | "Sorry this is late", "It's been a while" | Start. Nobody was counting. |
| AI tells | "In this week's edition, we delve into…", "Let's dive in!", an emoji on every heading, "Stay tuned!" teasers, an invented "Until next time!" | A plain first sentence, and the writer's own sign-off or none. |

## When the AI edits someone else's draft

- **The inbox lines are the writer's call.** The subject, the preview text, section names and any recurring
  greeting or sign-off stay exactly as written unless the writer's comment targets them; they are a format readers learned.
- **Merge tags are code.** `{{first_name}}`, `*|FNAME|*`, `{{ unsubscribe_url }}`, `[Unsubscribe]` and similar
  placeholders come back untouched, spelling and brackets included.
- **Links stay put.** Keep every URL, the link text the writer chose, the order of items in a list and the writer's
  take on each. Never add a link or a call to action.
- **Facts readers act on are verbatim.** Dates, times, time zones, prices, discount codes, deadlines and addresses
  don't move.
- **Keep who's talking.** Don't switch between "I" and "we", or from one reader to a crowd.
- **Line breaks are layout.** In email a paragraph break is design: keep the writer's breaks, short lines and
  one-line paragraphs unless the comment is about them.
- **Keep the format.** Don't turn a letter into bullets or a list into prose, and don't add headers, emoji, a
  greeting or a sign-off the passage didn't have.
- **Running jokes stay.** A callback to an earlier issue is the relationship with the reader, not a clarity problem.

## Standing instructions

Short lines a writer can keep in their own brief or style notes, so that an assistant applies them every
time. Each one is a habit drawn from the standards above, phrased in the writer's voice; keep the few that
fit the project.

- Make the subject line name one concrete thing inside the issue.
- Never repeat the subject line in the preview text.
- Give every link a sentence or two on why it's worth the click.
- Keep the same section names and order in every issue.
- One idea per issue; everything else goes under it, shorter.
- Ask for replies only with one specific question.
- Write dates, not "today" or "this morning"; people read it later.
- Never apologize for being late or quiet; just start.
