# Books & Fiction

Fiction readers come to live inside someone else's experience, and they want to do the inferring themselves:
what a character feels, what she wants, what the silence in a conversation means. They need what Gardner
called a vivid and continuous dream, one nothing interrupts. What goes wrong most often is an editor, human or
model, doing the reader's work for them: naming the feeling the action already carried, stating the theme,
settling the tension the writer meant to leave open.

## The standards

Items marked *(core)* are the short list to hand a writer or a model; the rest are the wider craft.

1. **Dramatize what changes; summarize what connects** *(core)*. A turning point told in two lines of
   summary is a missing scene; a drive across town told beat by beat is a scene that should be one sentence.
   Proportion is how the writer tells the reader what matters (Browne & King).
2. **Every scene turns.** By its last line something differs: what a character knows, who holds power, a
   relationship, a decision. A scene where nothing turned needs cutting or a turn, never prettier sentences.
3. **Enter late, leave early.** Start on motion, a want or something off; cut the arrival, the greetings and
   the aftermath that settles every account.
   - Before: *Mara got out of the car, walked up the drive and rang the bell. Her sister opened the door.*
   - After: *"You sold the piano," Mara said, before the door was all the way open.*
4. **Stay inside the point of view.** The POV character reports only what they perceive, know and would notice.
   Everyone else shows through behavior. A knowledge slip or a hop into another head wakes the reader up.
5. **Cut the filter, keep the thing.** *She noticed two snakes fighting* becomes *Two snakes were fighting*
   (Gardner's example). Keep *saw*, *heard*, *felt* only when the act of perceiving is itself the point.
6. **Resist the urge to explain** *(core)*. The sentence after the action that names the emotion, the
   motive or the lesson subtracts from it; Browne & King's margin note is 1 + 1 = ½. This covers the narrator
   stating the theme, which models do far more often than people (77% of stories against 52%, StoryScope).
7. **Name a feeling plainly or give it to behavior only this person would do; never to a stock body cue.**
   *Her heart pounded* belongs to every character in every book. *She was afraid* is honest; *She checked the
   lock, then checked it again* is hers. Models render emotion through bodily sensation in about four stories
   of five, people in fewer than two (StoryScope, as digested by sepia).
8. **Dialogue is a negotiation, not a briefing.** Each speaker wants something the other will not hand over, so
   they deflect, answer a different question, interrupt. Nobody says what both of them already know.
9. **Keep the writer's dialogue rhythm exactly** *(core)*. Fragments, false starts, repetition and
   dialect are characterization. Cleaning up a character's grammar deletes the character.
10. **"Said" is invisible; use it.** Tag with *said* or *asked*, or with an action beat; no *hissed*, *intoned*
    or *murmured*, and no adverb on *said* (Leonard's rules 3 and 4). Rotating synonyms for *said* is a
    machine habit; repeating *said* is a human one.
11. **Exposition arrives under pressure.** Give the consequence now and the explanation later, about one new
    world fact per scene, ideally inside a conflict. A passage that only moves facts from the notes to the
    reader is an info dump, however well written.
12. **One exact detail beats three adequate ones.** *A gull*, not *a bird*; *cold chips*, not *food*. Choose the
    detail the POV character would notice (a chef smells, a soldier counts exits) and drop the third sense in
    a three-sense sentence.
13. **Let tension persist.** No reconciliation beat after the fight, no silver lining after the loss, no closing
    line that sums the scene up. An argument may stay open for eight chapters or forever.
14. **Continuity outranks polish** *(core)*. Names, spellings, ages, the timeline, where objects are and
    who knows what stay as written unless a note says otherwise. A contradiction is reported, never silently
    resolved in favor of one version.
15. **Voices differ by syntax and motive, not by catchphrase.** Sentence length, register, what a character
    steers toward under stress and what they refuse to discuss. Test: cover the tags and see if the speaker is
    still clear.

## How a piece is built

**Before a scene is drafted or rewritten**, know four things: whose point of view it is, what that person wants
in this scene, what stands in the way, and how it comes out. The strongest outcomes complicate: *yes, but* or
*no, and*. A run of clean *yes* outcomes drains a book (story-skills' scene model, after Dwight Swain). After a
high-pressure scene, a short sequel (reaction, dilemma, decision) gives the reader room to feel what happened.

**The shape by length and genre.** The scene is the unit everywhere; what changes is what the reader came for.

| Form | What the reader came for | What an edit must protect |
|---|---|---|
| Novel | A world and an arc sustained over time; a reason to turn each chapter | Continuity across chapters; chapter-end pull |
| Short story | One movement toward one turn; entry very late | Compression, the final image, what is left unsaid |
| Flash (under ~1,500 words) | A single charged moment; the title does work | Every word, the ambiguity of the last line |
| Mystery and crime | Fair play: the clues are on the page | Planted clues and red herrings, even ones that look like clutter |
| Romance | Felt connection and the obstacle between two people | The tension; the genre's promised ending |
| Thriller and suspense | Threat present, time short, escalation | Pace, short scenes, the cliffhanger |
| Fantasy and science fiction | Rules shown through consequence | Invented words spelled one way; no lectures |
| Literary fiction | Interior life, language, ambiguity | The strangeness; deliberate roughness; open endings |

**Openings.** The first page owes the reader three things, in order: a voice that is unmistakably this
narrator's, a character who wants something now, and something wrong or about to change (story-skills'
first-page check). It should not lead with landscape, backstory, a dream, waking up, a mirror or the weather.
By the end of page one the reader can say who, where and roughly when. Drafts often start a page or two before
the real beginning: the first sentence where the writing comes alive in concrete detail is usually it.

**Chapter endings** pull forward: a question raised, a complication, a reversal, a decision. Three chapters in a
row that end on resolution flatten a book.

**Story endings** come one beat earlier than feels complete. Be wary of the default shape where the protagonist,
having grown, chooses the outcome and makes peace with it; StoryScope found models choose that combination far
more than people do. Chance, other people or institutions can decide an outcome; an ending can be partial, open
or bad. End on an image or an act, not on a sentence about what it meant.

## The editing pass

- [ ] Each scene: what is different at the end? If nothing, flag it; do not polish it.
- [ ] The first paragraph opens on motion, want or instability, and the reader can place who, where and when.
- [ ] Point of view holds: no head-hopping, no knowledge the POV character could not have.
- [ ] Filter verbs (*saw*, *noticed*, *felt*, *realized*, *watched as*) cut where perceiving is not the point.
- [ ] Every sentence that explains an emotion, motive or theme the scene already showed is gone.
- [ ] Emotion beats sorted: stock body cues converted to character-specific behavior or a plain statement.
- [ ] Dialogue tags are *said*, *asked* or an action beat; no adverb rides on *said*.
- [ ] No "as you know" dialogue; no lecture between two characters who share the facts.
- [ ] Exposition passes the cut test (does the scene still work without it?) and the knowledge test (does
      the POV character have a reason to think this now?).
- [ ] Details are specific, chosen by the POV character's attention, and rarely more than one per beat.
- [ ] Names, spellings, ages, dates and objects match the rest of the draft; contradictions are reported.
- [ ] Read aloud: three or more sentences of the same length in a row get broken by moving words, not adding.
- [ ] The sweep in "What goes wrong" below finds no cluster of tells.
- [ ] Deletion test on everything added: strike it; if the sentence still says the same thing, it was filler.

## What goes wrong

| Pattern | What it looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| The theme said aloud | *In the end, she understood that home was never a place.* | Cut it. End on the last concrete act. |
| The stock body | Pounding hearts, clenched jaws, a breath she didn't know she was holding | Behavior only this character would do, or name the feeling once. |
| Pathetic fallacy on repeat | Storm clouds gather every time the marriage strains | Let weather be weather; keep the one image that earns it. |
| Synonyms for *said* | *she breathed*, *he intoned*, *she murmured softly* | *said*, or an action beat that shows how it was said. |
| "As you know, Bob" | Two scientists explain the reactor to each other | Cut it, or give one of them a reason to withhold or dispute the fact. |
| The info dump | A paragraph on the tier system before anyone is in danger | Show the consequence; explain later, inside a conflict. |
| Everyone articulate | The teenager, the farmer and the professor all speak in balanced sentences | Fragments, deflection, wrong words, silence; differ by syntax. |
| The early reconciliation | The fight ends in a hug on the same page | Leave it open; the next scene can carry it. |
| Filtering | *She heard the door creak open and saw a shadow fall across the floor* | *The door creaked. A shadow fell across the floor.* |
| The tidy causal chain | Every event caused by the one before, a straight line to the climax | Leave a thread or a detail that does not pay off; let something arrive from offstage. |
| The opening pan | Two paragraphs of landscape before a person with a problem | Start where someone wants something. |
| Generic grandeur | *A testament to*, *the weight of*, *hung in the air*, *barely above a whisper* | Say the specific thing, or nothing. |
| The fiction-slop shelf | *Ozone*, *petrichor*, *gossamer*, *eyes glinting*, *shivers down her spine* | Replace with what this POV character would actually register. |
| Default names | Elara, Kael, Lyra, a surname like Voss or Vance | Keep the writer's names; never suggest these. |

A single item from this table is not a verdict; clusters are. One em dash, one plain *she was afraid*, one
abstract noun is ordinary prose (sepia's calibration rule: aim at the human band, do not invert every tell).

## When the AI edits someone else's draft

- Rewrite only the passage in question and keep it the same story: no new events, characters, backstory, names or
  world rules unless the note asks for them. When a note asks for something only the writer knows (a name, what
  happened next), leave the passage as written.
- Treat every name, invented word, spelling, tense, person and point of view in the passage as canon, even when
  it looks like a mistake. If two facts in the passage contradict, keep both; the writer resolves it.
- Keep the dialogue conventions the draft uses: dialogue introduced by a dash instead of quotation marks stays
  that way, single quotes stay single quotes, a dash for interruptions stays a dash. Never convert one system to
  another.
- Never correct a character's grammar, dialect or register, and never raise a clumsy narrator into a fluent one.
  A deliberately bad poem, letter or speech by a character stays bad.
- Leave objects, names and odd details in place even when they look like clutter. An editor handed one passage can't see
  chapter twenty, where that gun on the mantel may fire.
- When the note says "show, don't tell", convert with behavior drawn from this character and this scene, not a
  stock gesture, and do not add sensory detail the surrounding text contradicts.
- Match the writer's sentence length, paragraphing and punctuation habits in the paragraphs around it, so
  the edit cannot be found by its seams.

## Standing instructions

Short lines a writer can keep in their own brief or style notes, so that an assistant applies them every
time. Each one is a habit drawn from the standards above, phrased in the writer's voice; keep the few that
fit the project.

- Tag dialogue with "said" or an action beat; never "hissed", "intoned" or "murmured".
- Never open a chapter with weather, waking up or a character looking in a mirror.
- Cut filter verbs: show what she sees, not that she saw it.
- No stock body cues: no pounding hearts, clenched jaws or held breaths.
- Never end a scene on a sentence that sums up what it meant.
- Stay in one point of view per scene; never reveal what that character cannot know.
- Start scenes as late as possible and cut the goodbyes.
- Name the exact thing: a gull, not a bird; cold chips, not food.
