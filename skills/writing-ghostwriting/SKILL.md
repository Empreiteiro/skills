# Ghostwriting

Two readers judge this kind of writing: the author's audience, who believe they are hearing the author, and
the author, who has to recognise every sentence as theirs before it ships. Both need the author's ideas,
experiences and way of speaking, not the ghost's and not the model's. The commonest failure is a
draft better written than the author writes: fluent, balanced, tidy, and therefore nobody's, with two
of the author's catchphrases sprinkled on top.

## The standards

1. **The author's own words are the style guide.** Read their unassisted writing, and better still
   transcripts of them talking, before drafting a line. A habit enters the voice sheet only when you
   can quote it twice from different pieces; one occurrence is an accident, and a description you
   would have to argue for ("warm", "no-nonsense") is not a rule.
2. **The spoken voice outranks the written one.** Interviews, voice memos, talks and calls keep the
   rhythm that editors have already sanded off published pieces, which is why professional ghosts
   record every session and value the early, unguarded ones most. When the spoken and written voice
   disagree, ask the author which one this piece should sound like.
3. **Match the rate at which a habit appears.** If the author says "frankly" once a piece, it appears
   once, not in every paragraph. A quirk at full volume turns into caricature, the most common
   failure of imitation; the author's own frequency is a ceiling.
   - Before (caricature): every post opens "Quick one:" → After: one post in five does, as in the samples.
4. **Keep their side of every word pair.** When the author consistently picks "use" over "utilise",
   "but" over "however", "folks" over "people", "a lot" over "significantly", the pick stays. Authorship
   studies since Mosteller and Wallace's work on the Federalist Papers tell writers apart by exactly
   these small, topic-free words, and a reader's ear does the same.
   - Before: "However, we subsequently realised the approach was insufficient."
   - After, for an author who writes "but", "then" and "didn't work": "But then we saw it didn't work."
5. **Their certainty, not yours.** Keep "I think", "probably" and "kind of" where they use them, and
   keep a flat verdict flat. Added hedges, both-sides balance and caveats make an author sound like a
   committee; stripped hedges make them sound like someone louder.
6. **Only the author's experiences, opinions and numbers.** Every anecdote, client, quote, figure, date
   and feeling comes from the author or their material. A gap becomes `[ASK AUTHOR: …]`, never a
   plausible invention, because the author will be asked about it on stage, in an interview or in the
   comments.
7. **Reproduce how they think, too.** Note how the author opens (a story, a number, a thesis), argues
   (a counterexample, data, an analogy from their trade), treats the other side, and ends (a call to
   act, an open question, a one-line verdict). Right sentences in the wrong arc read
   more foreign than slightly wrong sentences in the author's arc.
8. **Metaphors come from the author's world.** A surgeon's images come from the operating theatre and
   a farmer's from the seasons; the ghost's references, and the model's favourite journeys and
   landscapes, don't belong. A field the author never draws on is part of the fingerprint too.
9. **Voice travels across channels; format doesn't.** The same author writes a chapter, a LinkedIn post
   and an op-ed with the same words and stance in different shapes. Hard form conventions win first (a
   newspaper's length and style, a publisher's house style), then the audience, then the author's
   habits, then the platform's; never carry one channel's quirks into another.
10. **Keep the rough edges that are theirs.** Fragments, sentences starting with And or But, the same
    word repeated instead of varied, regional spelling and dialect, profanity at their rate. An editor
    would "fix" these; a ghost protects them. Don't move British spelling to American or the reverse.
11. **Don't write jokes in someone else's name.** Humour is the hardest part of a voice to fake and the
    most embarrassing to get wrong. Mark the spot (`[room for the author's aside?]`) and let the author
    write it.
12. **Lay the voice over plain prose, never over model defaults.** Strip the generic machine habits
    first (tidy triads, "moreover", evenly sized paragraphs, contrast reveals, recap endings), then add
    the author's patterns. An author who genuinely uses dashes or triads keeps them, at their rate.
13. **Don't upgrade the author.** Not smarter vocabulary, not warmer, not more certain, not more
    quotable than they are. A voice the author can't sustain in a live Q&A or a reply thread exposes
    both of you.
14. **The author approves every claim and every "I".** Hand back the draft with a short note of what
    came from their words and what was inferred, plus every `[ASK AUTHOR]`. Agree with the author up
    front which tools, AI included, will touch the work, as the ghostwriting industry's 2026 AI
    guidelines ask.

## How a piece is built

**First, the voice sheet.** Collect a few thousand words across at least two registers (one polished,
one casual), none of it already rewritten by AI, plus transcripts if they exist. Read the whole set
before writing anything down, fill the sheet with quoted evidence, and show it to the author to
correct before drafting. A thin sample gives a tentative sheet: say so and revisit it.

| Dimension | Capture | A typical entry |
|---|---|---|
| Rhythm | Usual sentence length, how much it varies, recurring shapes | "Two short sentences, then a long one that works it out" |
| Openers | How pieces and paragraphs start; literal repeated phrases | "Opens three of five posts on a number" |
| Closers | How pieces end | "A one-line verdict; never a question" |
| Certainty | Which hedge words, and whether they hedge then commit or commit then concede | "'I think' before verdicts; never 'perhaps'" |
| Word pairs | The diagnostic choices, counted | "but/however 14 to 0; use/utilise 9 to 0" |
| Signature phrases | Phrases that recur across pieces, with counts | "'the boring part' in four pieces" |
| Metaphor fields | Which domains, and which are absent | "Kitchens and football; never war" |
| Punctuation and format | Dashes, brackets, semicolons, exclamation marks, lists, numbers as digits or words | "Asides in brackets, about one per post" |
| Humour | The mechanism, or none | "Self-deprecating detail, never wordplay" |
| Not errors | Deliberate "mistakes" to protect | "Starts sentences with And; repeats 'simple' on purpose" |
| Never says | Words, formulas and moves that would ring false | "'excited to announce', exclamation marks, 'journey'" |

**Then, the material.** Interview for substance, not style: the specific moment ("walk me through the
day it broke"), the numbers, the honest trade-off, the position they would defend in an argument, what
they are unsure of, and the exact words a customer or colleague used. Record it; the recording is both
the source of facts and the best voice sample you will get.

**Shapes by kind of piece.**

| Piece | How it is built | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Book or memoir | Chapter by chapter from interview transcripts, as the author telling one story to one listener; the arc agreed in an outline first | The ghost's literary register creeping in; invented scenes and dialogue |
| Op-ed or bylined article | Thesis in the first paragraph, the author's own evidence, the publication's length and style | House style decides mechanics; the author decides wording |
| Executive or founder posts | Their opinion, their week, their numbers; one idea per post; their approval before anything goes out | "As a CEO, I've learned…" thought-leadership templates |
| Newsletter | The series' established structure, recurring sections and sign-off | Drift from what earlier issues taught readers to expect |
| Letter, memo or e-mail in their name | Their greeting, their sign-off, the ask where they usually put it | Letter formulas they never use ("I hope this finds you well") |
| Speech for a named speaker | The voice comes from here; the performance rules from `writing-scripts-speeches` | Lines they cannot say aloud without stumbling |

**Drafting.** Where transcripts exist, build outward from them: lift the author's own sentences for the
key points, then write only the connective tissue, in their rhythm. Open and close the way they
usually do. Hand back the draft, the sourced-versus-inferred note and the `[ASK AUTHOR]` list.

## The editing pass

- [ ] Every anecdote, number, name, quote and opinion traces to the author's words or material; the rest is marked `[ASK AUTHOR]`.
- [ ] Side by side with a sample from the same channel, sentence length, punctuation, openings and closings match.
- [ ] Word pairs follow the voice sheet; no elevated word where the author uses a plain one.
- [ ] Signature phrases appear at the author's rate, not more.
- [ ] Hedges and verdicts sit at the author's level of certainty; no added balance or caveats.
- [ ] Metaphors come only from the author's fields.
- [ ] The argument moves the way the author's arguments move.
- [ ] Deliberate rough edges (fragments, And/But openings, repetition, dialect, spelling) survived.
- [ ] No model defaults: tidy triads, "moreover", contrast reveals, recap endings, chatbot sign-offs.
- [ ] No joke written in the author's name; humour spots are flagged.
- [ ] The channel's conventions applied without importing another channel's quirks.
- [ ] Read aloud as the author: nothing they would stumble on or refuse to say.
- [ ] Inferred choices are listed for the author to approve.

## What goes wrong

| Pattern | Looks like | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Model leak | Even paragraphs, tidy transitions, "moreover", three-item lists everywhere | Find the messier spots in the author's samples and follow those |
| Caricature | The catchphrase in every paragraph | Cut to the author's frequency |
| Persona invention | An anecdote, credential or opinion the author never gave | `[ASK AUTHOR: …]` |
| Polish creep | "utilise", "subsequently", semicolons from an author who writes "use", "then" and full stops | The author's plain words and punctuation |
| Topic drift | Hiking metaphors in a tax piece because the samples were about hiking | Style comes from the samples; subject matter doesn't |
| Channel contamination | LinkedIn line breaks in a book chapter; Slack lowercase in an op-ed | Each channel's own shape, the same voice |
| Committee thinking | "Both approaches have merit" from an author who always picks a side | Their verdict, stated their way |
| Template wisdom | "As a founder, here are three things I've learned about leadership:" | The one specific moment that taught it |
| Quote polishing | A colleague's or customer's words tidied into grammatical prose | Verbatim, as said |
| The ghost's own voice | The same favourite constructions turning up across every client | Check against the author's sheet, not the ghost's taste |
| Fake humanising | Slang, typos or lowercase added "to sound real" | Casual only where the author's samples are casual |

## When the AI edits someone else's draft

- The voice to match is the named author's: not the person at the keyboard and not the model. The
  text around the passage is the nearest sample, so take rhythm, punctuation and vocabulary
  from it.
- A word or structure the author repeats is voice, even when a style rule, the AI-tell list included,
  would flag it. Keep it.
- Apply the note by the author's means. "More formal" for a casual author means full sentences and
  fewer asides in their own words, not borrowed corporate vocabulary.
- Add no fact, anecdote, name, number, credential or opinion. When a note asks for "a story here" or
  "a stat", insert `[ASK AUTHOR: …]` where it would go.
- Keep spelling variety, dialect, profanity and punctuation habits as they are; introduce no dash,
  semicolon or exclamation mark the author doesn't use.
- Change no certainty: no added hedge, no softened verdict, no new caveat.
- Quotes from other people, names and dates stay verbatim.

## Standing instructions

Short lines a writer can keep in their own brief or style notes, so that an assistant applies them every
time. Each one is a habit drawn from the standards above, phrased in the writer's voice; keep the few that
fit the project.

- Never invent an anecdote, number or opinion; write [ASK AUTHOR] instead.
- Take metaphors only from the author's own work and life.
- Use the author's pet phrases at their usual rate, never more.
- Keep the author's spelling and dialect; don't normalise it.
- Open and close the way the author usually does.
- No jokes in the author's name; mark the spot for them to fill.
- Keep the author's own words for their company, product and customers.
- Build from the author's transcripts first; write only what connects them.
